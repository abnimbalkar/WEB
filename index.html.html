MILE 1

01 A Brief History of Cycling

The story begins in 1817, when German inventor Karl von Drais unveiled the "Laufmaschine," or running machine — a wooden, two-wheeled contraption steered by handlebars but propelled by the rider's feet pushing directly against the ground. It had no pedals, no chain, and no brakes, yet it planted the seed for everything that followed.

Pedals arrived in the 1860s courtesy of French inventors, giving rise to the "boneshaker," so named for the bone-rattling ride delivered by its iron-and-wood frame over cobblestone streets. The 1870s brought the high-wheeled "penny-farthing," with its oversized front wheel offering greater speed at the cost of stability, and no shortage of dramatic tumbles.

The real breakthrough came in 1885 with John Kemp Starley's "safety bicycle," featuring two similarly sized wheels, a chain-driven rear wheel, and a diamond frame — the same basic layout still used today. Combined with John Boyd Dunlop's pneumatic tire in 1888, the bicycle became comfortable, efficient, and accessible. By the 1890s, a full-blown "bicycle craze" had swept Europe and America, reshaping fashion, mobility, and even social norms, as bicycles gave unprecedented independence to women of the era.

Through the twentieth century, cycling split into many branches: road racing traditions like the Tour de France (first held in 1903), utilitarian city cycling across the Netherlands and Denmark, and later mountain biking, BMX, and gravel riding. Through it all, the core machine has changed remarkably little — a triumph of early mechanical design that still carries hundreds of millions of people every day.

MILE 2

02 The Benefits of Cycling

More than two centuries after its invention, the bicycle remains one of the most efficient machines ever built for converting human effort into motion — and one of the best things a person can do for their body, their wallet, and their surroundings.

  • Cardiovascular HealthRegular cycling strengthens the heart, improves circulation, and lowers resting heart rate, reducing the risk of heart disease and stroke over time.
  • Low-Impact ExerciseUnlike running, cycling is easy on the joints while still building leg strength, endurance, and core stability — suitable for nearly all ages and fitness levels.
  • Mental WellbeingTime in the saddle releases endorphins, reduces stress and anxiety, and the rhythmic, repetitive motion has a calming, almost meditative effect.
  • Weight ManagementA steady commute or leisure ride burns significant calories, helping maintain a healthy weight without the wear and tear of higher-impact workouts.
  • Environmental ImpactA bicycle produces zero emissions, eases traffic congestion, and requires a fraction of the road space and materials of a car.
  • Economic SavingsNo fuel, minimal maintenance, and no parking fees — cycling is one of the cheapest forms of transportation available, with a bike often paying for itself within months.
  • LongevityLarge population studies link regular cycling to a longer life expectancy and reduced risk of premature death from any cause.
  • Community & FreedomFrom club rides to solo commutes, the bicycle offers an unmatched sense of independence and connects riders to their surroundings in a way few other vehicles can.
